OPTIONAL: THE CASTLE CHALLENGE

Two quick strategy games, about 3 minutes in all. This is optional, but top
scorers get priority registration for the tournament.

You have 100 soldiers to split across 5 castles, worth 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5
points. Whole numbers only, adding up to exactly 100.


GAME 1: HEAD TO HEAD

QUESTION
Your allocation is matched against every other applicant's. In each matchup,
whoever sends more soldiers to a castle wins its points; a tie splits them.
Your score is your average across all matchups.

How would you allocate your soldiers?

ANSWER
Castle 1 (1 point):   1 soldier
Castle 2 (2 points):  2 soldiers
Castle 3 (3 points): 30 soldiers
Castle 4 (4 points): 31 soldiers
Castle 5 (5 points): 36 soldiers
Total:             100 soldiers

EXPLANATION
The objective is to maximize average castle points, not the number of
head-to-head matchups won. An 8-7 result earns 8 points, while a 12-3 result
earns 12 points.

This allocation concentrates on castles 3-5, which together are worth 12
points. It earns 12 points against both an even allocation of 20 soldiers
per castle and the proportional allocation 7/13/20/27/33.

The 1 and 2 soldiers on the smaller castles collect their points against
players who leave those castles empty. Against 0/0/33/33/34, for example,
this allocation wins castles 1, 2 and 5 for a total of 8 points.

The amounts 31 and 36 also beat allocations of 30 and 35 on their respective
castles. This relies on an assumption that round-number choices will be
common; we do not have evidence about the actual applicants' allocations.

This is a heuristic allocation, not a proven optimum. Concentrated opponents
can beat it. Against 0/0/0/50/50, for example, it earns only 6 points from
castles 1, 2 and 3.


GAME 2: FOUR-WAY BATTLE

QUESTION
Same castles, same 100 soldiers, but now you are in a random group of 4
applicants. Each castle goes to whoever sends the most soldiers to it;
tied castles split their points. Players are ranked by total castle points,
and only your finish counts:

1st: 9 points
2nd: 4 points
3rd: 2 points
4th: 0 points

Players tied in the rankings split the payouts for those places. Groups
are reshuffled thousands of times, and your results are averaged.

How would you allocate your soldiers?

ANSWER
Castle 1 (1 point):   0 soldiers
Castle 2 (2 points):  0 soldiers
Castle 3 (3 points): 29 soldiers
Castle 4 (4 points): 33 soldiers
Castle 5 (5 points): 38 soldiers
Total:             100 soldiers

EXPLANATION
The objective changes to maximizing expected placement payout. First place
pays 9 points, compared with 4 for second, so strategies should be evaluated
by how they affect the probabilities of all four finishing positions.

This allocation moves the soldiers from castles 1 and 2 onto the higher-value
castles and shifts one soldier from castle 3 toward castles 4 and 5. The
intent is to improve the chance of securing a first-place combination.

Winning castles 3 and 5 outright gives 8 castle points and guarantees outright
first place: the other three players can share only the remaining 7 points.
Winning castles 4 and 5 outright gives 9 and also guarantees outright first.
Winning castles 3 and 4 gives only 7, so that pair does not guarantee first.
These guarantees require winning the castles outright; sharing a tied castle
reduces the points received.

The tradeoff is exposure to opponents who commit more soldiers to the same
castles. This allocation may finish poorly against those players. The payout
structure makes first-place chances valuable, but it does not establish that
this exact allocation, or greater concentration in general, is optimal.
That depends on the opponents' allocations.


STRATEGY QUESTION

How did you approach each game, and did your strategy change?

ANSWER TO PASTE

In Game 1, I aimed to maximize expected castle points. I concentrated on
castles 3-5 to beat evenly spread or proportional allocations, while keeping
small amounts on castles 1 and 2 to collect points against players who left
them empty. I chose amounts just above several round-number thresholds,
assuming those would be common choices.

In Game 2, I shifted toward competing for first place because the payout
rises sharply from second to first. I moved the remaining soldiers onto the
three highest-value castles. Winning castles 3 and 5 outright would guarantee
first place, as would winning castles 4 and 5. This accepts the risk of
finishing poorly when opponents concentrate more heavily on the same castles.
